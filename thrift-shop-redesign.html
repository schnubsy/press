Thrift Shop: a config-first redesign

Six directions, each anchored to a named movement and Flow-reviewed, scored on a weighting built for this project. Every mockup is live: tap the tabs, open and close sections (state is remembered per section), toggle switches, tap values to cycle them, search, switch light and dark, and switch phone and desktop.

A. The project story

Thrift Shop promises Mark one thing: it watches gift-card feeds, store pages, his Outlook offers folder and a handful of product searches, and speaks up only when a deal beats his rules. The feed is the reward, but the job Mark actually does in the app is operating the agent: about 60 brand rules, 20 sources with five health states, sale-watch terms, eight email-offer types, alert timing, and lists of cash-like and blocked stores. The app grew into a configuration console without ever being designed as one. So this review designs a console first, keeps the Feed as home, and treats every value Mark reads (a last-match title, a % threshold, a source's state) as content that must be fully visible and never truncated.

Detected: vanilla HTML/CSS/JS single page (thrift-shop.html, press Lane A, no build step), iPhone-first with desktop. Tokens below are emitted as CSS custom properties; no Tailwind. Functional inventory came from docs/spec.md and the page's labels and controls only. The current CSS, layout and nav were deliberately not consulted.

Steer used (from the brief, standing in for the intake meeting): collapsible sections with remembered state, dense label/value/control rows, help only on demand, few top-level destinations with the Feed as home, nothing truncated, a tight grid, compact controls on 44px targets, health readable at a glance, 390px first plus desktop, light and dark.

Thrift Shop is a personal daily-driver, which the default table would weight 50% on aesthetics. That would reward exactly the problem Mark described. Here the four complaints carry 80% of the weight, and aesthetics drops to 10%.

B. Six directions

All six share one functional model, so they can be compared like for like and blended: Feed (Inbox and Processed), Gift-card rules (defaults, four brand groups, cash-like, blocked), Sales (sale watch, email offer types, brand overrides, also-count), Sources (Email, Feeds, Retailers, Marketplaces, five health states) and Alerts/Settings (channels, quiet hours and digest, agent health, schedule, savings, Bad backlog, learned rules). Every section header collapses its section. Help is behind a ? on each header.

Why each criterion scored as it did

CriterionJustification
Density & scannabilitySteel (10) and Grid (9) fit sixty brand rules into a single aligned table of 36–40px rows, and Grid's column header lets the eye run straight down Min %. Cupertino (7) pays for iOS insets. Calm (5) and Vogue (4) trade density for calm and drama by design.
Navigation clarityGrid's three nouns (Feed, Rules, System) and Cupertino's tab bar (9 each) give every destination one job. Calm's labelled bottom bar scores 8. Steel (7) relies on Go for depth, which is slower one-handed. Neon's capsule (7) floats over content. Vogue's single Index (6) brings back the scrolling Mark complained about.
Readability of key valuesGrid and Calm (9) wrap last-match text in full in proportional type and right-align values in tabular figures. Cupertino (8) sets values in secondary grey. Steel's mono values (7) are crisp but wide. Neon's glass (6) costs contrast at the edges.
Config ergonomicsGrid and Cupertino (9): tap a value to change it in place, switch at the edge, search plus collapse on every tab. Steel (8) adds the Go jump. Calm (7) adds an extra open step. Neon and Vogue (6, 5) put more travel between Mark and a value.
AestheticsVogue (9) has the strongest moment. Neon and Calm (8) are distinctive. Grid and Cupertino (7) are disciplined but quiet. Steel (6) is honest and cold.
AccessibilityCalm and Cupertino (9) have the highest contrast and the most forgiving targets. Grid (8). Neon (5): glass and glow need careful work.
FeasibilityEvery direction ships as vanilla CSS. Neon's backdrop-filter is the only real performance risk on older iPhone WebKit. Vogue's cover needs its own empty and quiet states.

D. Recommendation

    Winner: Price Index (Grid, Swiss/International). It answers every one of Mark's complaints directly rather than by mood. Sixty rules become one ruled table whose columns line up across every section. Values sit in the same right-hand column on every screen, so his eye learns one path. There are three nouns in the nav. Section headers are numbered addresses that fold and stay folded. Last-match text wraps in full. Red appears only when something needs him, so a red row always means act. It is the direction where the screen stops talking and starts listing.

    Runner-up: Native Ledger (Cupertino, iOS-native), at 0.65 behind. It is the one I would choose if Mark wants Thrift Shop to feel like part of the phone instead of a page on it: zero learning, adaptive light and dark for free, and a segmented Rules screen that cuts the longest scroll in half. It loses to Grid on density (iOS insets) and on identity. There is a real tension here: Grid wins on the rubric, while Cupertino is the more platform-native of the two. That is Mark's call, and the scores do not hide it.

    Optional hybrid: Grid's index over Calm's closed-section summaries. Keep Price Index's rows, three tabs and red signal, and borrow Still Water's one idea that no other direction has: a collapsed header that still states its values ("Restaurants 29, 20% min, except Starbucks 15%"). Mark could then audit his whole configuration with every section closed. Steel's Go jump is worth a later pass on desktop, where a keyboard is always to hand.
